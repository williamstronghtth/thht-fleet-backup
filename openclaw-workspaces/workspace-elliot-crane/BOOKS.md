## Superforecasting — Philip Tetlock
**Status:** ✅ COMPLETE (read cover to cover 2026-03-16)

### Core Philosophy
The book's central thesis: forecasting is a *skill* that can be cultivated through deliberate practice. The best forecasters (superforecasters) beat intelligence analysts with classified info by ~30% — not through brilliance, but through better *process*.

---

### Operational Changes Implemented

**1. Brier Score Tracking (Mandatory)**
- Every prediction logged with: my estimate, market price, outcome, my Brier, market Brier
- Formula: Brier = (probability - outcome)² where outcome = 0 or 1
- Lower is better. Perfect = 0, random = 0.25, always wrong = 1.0
- Track cumulative to verify I have actual edge

**2. No Weasel Words (Hard Rule)**
- Ban: "could," "might," "may," "possible" without numbers
- Every forecast: specific outcome + numeric probability + time bound
- Wrong: "CPI could be high"
- Right: "CPI >0.7% by Apr 10: 70%"

**3. Outside View First (Reference Class Anchoring)**
- Start EVERY forecast from base rates, then adjust
- Ask: "How often has X happened historically?" before inside-view analysis
- Resist the pull of "this time is different"

**4. Fermi-ize Everything**
- Break complex questions into smaller, estimable components
- Combine multiple approaches and average results
- "Dare to be wrong" at each step — crude guesses that compound are better than paralysis

**5. Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis**
- For every trade: write the bull case, then *seriously* argue the bear case
- Not performative devil's advocacy — genuinely consider "why might I be wrong?"
- Seek disconfirming evidence with the same rigor as confirming

**6. Granularity Matters**
- Use precise probabilities, not buckets (52% not "likely")
- Track whether my granularity is calibrated — am I distinguishing 60% from 70% accurately?
- Be wary of false precision — update range if uncertain about distribution

**7. Small, Frequent Updates**
- Bayesian updating: many small adjustments beat infrequent large swings
- Set triggers: What news would move me +5%? What would move me -10%?
- Avoid belief perseverance — update promptly when evidence shifts

**8. "Perpetual Beta" Mindset**
- Every forecast is a hypothesis to test, not a position to defend
- Post-mortems on HITS as well as misses — was I right for the right reasons?
- Track what factors actually predicted outcomes vs. what I *thought* would matter

**9. Aggregation & Crowd Wisdom**
- My estimate should weight: my analysis + market price + other smart forecasters
- "Extremizing" — when aggregating diverse views, move further toward consensus direction
- Recognize prediction markets ARE crowd wisdom — beating them requires genuine edge

**10. Time Horizon Discipline**
- Short-range (<1 year): skill matters, track performance
- Long-range (>3-5 years): essentially speculation, weight accordingly
- "Forecasting horizon" — beyond certain timeframes, unpredictability dominates

---

### New Trading Rules Added

**R1. Pre-Trade Checklist (expanded)**
Before ANY trade:
- [ ] Base rate established (outside view)
- [ ] Inside view factors listed
- [ ] Thesis written
- [ ] Antithesis written (genuine devil's advocate)
- [ ] Combined synthesis → probability estimate
- [ ] Update triggers defined (what moves me ±5%?)
- [ ] Exit conditions specified

**R2. Update Schedule**
- Check positions daily for new info
- Log any probability update >3 points with reasoning
- Avoid overtrading — updates should be evidence-driven, not noise-driven

**R3. Post-Mortem Protocol**
After every resolution:
- Was I right/wrong?
- Was market right/wrong?
- Was I right for the RIGHT reasons? (key insight)
- What would I do differently?
- Add to Brier tracking

**R4. Cognitive Bias Checklist**
Before trading:
- [ ] WYSIATI check: What info am I missing?
- [ ] Scope insensitivity: Am I scaling probabilities correctly across time/events?
- [ ] Confirmation bias: Have I sought disconfirming evidence?
- [ ] Belief perseverance: Am I holding a view despite contrary evidence?

## Fortune's Formula — William Poundstone
**Status:** ✅ COMPLETE (read cover to cover 2026-03-16)

### Core Philosophy
The book traces the intellectual history of the Kelly criterion from Claude Shannon's information theory through John Kelly Jr.'s gambling formula to Ed Thorp's blackjack and hedge fund empires. Central thesis: **The Kelly criterion is the mathematical answer to "how much should I bet?"** — it maximizes long-term wealth growth while guaranteeing you never go broke.

---

### The Kelly Formula

**Basic Formula:**
```
f* = edge / odds

Where:
- f* = fraction of bankroll to bet
- edge = probability of winning minus probability of losing (as decimal)
- odds = payout ratio (what you win per dollar bet)

Example: 55% win rate, even money payout
- edge = 0.55 - 0.45 = 0.10
- odds = 1
- f* = 0.10 / 1 = 10% of bankroll
```

**For Prediction Markets (Yes/No bets):**
```
f* = (p × b - q) / b

Where:
- p = your estimated probability of YES
- q = 1 - p (probability of NO)  
- b = net odds (payout minus 1)

Or simplified for markets priced in cents:
- My estimate: 70¢ (70% YES probability)
- Market price: 50¢
- Expected profit if I buy YES at 50¢: (0.70 × 50¢) - (0.30 × 50¢) = 35¢ - 15¢ = 20¢
- Risk per contract: 50¢
- Kelly fraction: 20/50 = 40% of bankroll

BUT: Use HALF Kelly (20%) for safety!
```

---

### Operational Changes Implemented

**1. Half-Kelly Maximum (Hard Rule)**
- NEVER bet full Kelly — always use ½ Kelly or less
- Rationale: Half-Kelly cuts volatility drastically while only reducing return by ~25%
- Full Kelly: 1/3 chance of losing half your bankroll before doubling
- Half Kelly: 1/9 chance of the same catastrophe
- **Book quote:** "Most of the people who successfully use the Kelly criterion in fact aim for a bet or position size less than the Kelly bet"

**2. Fractional Kelly for Uncertainty (New Rule)**
- When probability estimates are uncertain → use ¼ Kelly or less
- Estimation error that makes you OVERBET is catastrophic
- Overbetting (>1× Kelly) produces WORSE returns than underbetting
- Ed Thorp: "Long term compounders should further limit their investment fraction enough to prevent a significant risk of overbetting"

**3. Leverage = Overbetting (Critical Insight)**
- LTCM used 25-30× leverage on tiny edges
- This pushed them past Kelly into "insane" territory
- **The book's key diagram:** Returns peak at Kelly, then decline as bet size increases
- Betting 2× Kelly produces ZERO compound return
- Betting >2× Kelly produces NEGATIVE compound return
- **I will NEVER use leverage in prediction markets**

**4. Diversification Reduces Volatility**
- Multiple uncorrelated bets smooth the ride
- Princeton-Newport: smooth exponential growth through hundreds of simultaneous bets
- Single serial bets: wild swings (50% chance of losing half before doubling)
- **Implication:** Spread across multiple uncorrelated markets when possible

**5. Correlation Kills Diversification**
- LTCM thought they had hundreds of bets — actually just a few correlated ones
- "Flight to quality" made all trades move together
- **My rule:** When markets become correlated (panic/crisis), reduce ALL positions

**6. VaR Is Insufficient**
- Value at Risk tells you likely losses, not worst-case
- Kelly asks: "What is the chance of losing EVERYTHING?"
- VaR sidesteps this question — dangerous
- **I need to think about ruin probability, not just expected loss**

**7. "Survival Motive" is Paramount**
- Kelly's paranoid conservatism: never risk ruin, ever
- Even astronomically unlikely ruin events must be avoided
- **Book quote:** "Even unlikely events must come to pass eventually. Therefore, anyone who accepts small risks of losing everything will lose everything, sooner or later."

**8. Martingale is Suicide**
- Doubling down after losses = guaranteed ruin with finite bankroll
- Meriwether was known as a "martingale man" — doubled down when losing
- LTCM let leverage drift UP as they lost money (exactly backwards!)
- **My rule:** NEVER increase position size after losses

**9. Shannon's Stock-Picking Philosophy**
- "Extraction of signals from noise" — same skill as information theory
- Technical analysis = "noisy reproduction of important data"
- Focus on earnings growth, not price momentum
- Visit companies, talk to management, try the products
- **Implication:** For prediction markets, focus on fundamentals (actual probabilities) not price charts

**10. Track Record Requires Decisions, Not Years**
- Statistical significance comes from NUMBER of decisions
- Thorp: "Approximately 1.25 million individual 'bets' averaging $65,000 each"
- A few lucky years means nothing; consistent edge across many decisions is signal
- **I must track every trade to build statistical evidence of edge**

---

### New Position Sizing Rules

**PS1. Kelly Calculation (Required Before Every Trade)**
```
Step 1: My probability estimate: ____%
Step 2: Market price: ____¢
Step 3: Edge = (My estimate - Market price) = ____%
Step 4: Full Kelly = Edge / (100 - Market price) × 100 = ___% of bankroll
Step 5: Half Kelly = Full Kelly / 2 = ___% of bankroll
Step 6: Position size = Bankroll × Half Kelly = $____
Step 7: Cap at $25 (hard limit) = $____
```

**PS2. Uncertainty Adjustment**
| Confidence in Estimate | Kelly Fraction |
|------------------------|----------------|
| Very high (strong model) | ½ Kelly |
| High (solid thesis) | ⅓ Kelly |
| Moderate (informed guess) | ¼ Kelly |
| Low (speculative) | ⅛ Kelly or skip |

**PS3. Never Overbet**
- Overbetting = any bet >1× Kelly
- Compound return DECREASES beyond Kelly
- At 2× Kelly, compound return = 0
- Past 2× Kelly, compound return goes NEGATIVE
- **This is the "bright clear line" between aggressive and insane**

**PS4. Correlation Check**
Before adding position, ask:
- How correlated is this to my existing positions?
- In a panic/crisis, would they all move together?
- If yes → reduce total exposure, don't add

---

### LTCM Lessons (Critical Case Study)

**What Went Wrong:**
1. **25-30× leverage** on tiny edges (0.67% expected profit)
2. **Zero haircuts** negotiated with lenders → infinite leverage on some trades
3. **Correlation blindness** — thought they had hundreds of bets, really had ~3 big bets
4. **VaR tunnel vision** — worst case shown was $2.5B loss (they lost $4.4B)
5. **Martingale mindset** — let leverage INCREASE as losses mounted
6. **No survival motive** — didn't ask "what can ruin us?"

**What To Learn:**
- Merton & Scholes (Nobel laureates!) lost ~$100M each
- "Ten or 15 guys with an average IQ of maybe 170" got wiped out
- **Intelligence doesn't protect against overbetting**
- Process > brains when it comes to position sizing

**The Thorp Contrast:**
- Princeton-Newport: 15.1% annual return after fees, 19 years
- Standard deviation: ~4% (less volatile than S&P 500!)
- S&P 500: 8.8% annual return over same period
- Beat market by 6+ points with LESS volatility
- **How:** Fractional Kelly, diversification, hedged trades

---

### Key Insights for Prediction Markets

**1. Small Edges × Many Bets = Big Money**
- Thorp's statistical arbitrage: 0.5% edge per trade, thousands of trades
- Hong Kong Syndicate: ~$100M/year from horse racing
- **Don't chase home runs — hunt for systematic small edges**

**2. Fees Matter More Than You Think**
- Thorp: "Half of that went to transaction costs"
- 0.5% edge, 0.25% profit after costs
- **At Kalshi: 2¢ round trip + spread = need ~5%+ edge minimum**

**3. The Market is Usually Right**
- Shannon: "Stock prices will, in the long run, follow earnings"
- Prediction market prices = crowd wisdom
- Beating them requires GENUINE edge, not wishful thinking
- **Always stress-test: "Why might the market be right?"**

**4. Logarithmic Utility is Correct**
- Doubling $100 → $200 feels as good as doubling $1M → $2M
- Losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good
- Kelly maximizes log(wealth), which matches human psychology
- **This is why "small consistent gains" beats "occasional big wins"**

---

### Academic Debate Settled (For My Purposes)

The book covers the Samuelson vs. Kelly debate. My conclusion:

**Samuelson's objection:** Kelly maximizes geometric mean, not expected utility for all utility functions. Some people are more/less risk-tolerant.

**Kelly response:** Most people's primary preference is "more money faster" — logarithmic utility fits this. Kelly also has unique properties (maximizes growth, guarantees no ruin).

**My position:** For a prediction market trader with long time horizon, Kelly (fractional) is the correct framework. The "utility function" objection is academic. I want maximum compound return without blowing up.

## Thinking, Fast and Slow — Daniel Kahneman
**Status:** ✅ COMPLETE (read cover to cover 2026-03-17)

### Core Philosophy
The book's central architecture: two modes of thinking govern all judgment and choice. System 1 (fast, automatic, intuitive) generates impressions that System 2 (slow, deliberate, lazy) usually endorses. Most biases originate in System 1, but System 2's laziness lets them through. **The path to better decisions: recognize when you're in a "cognitive minefield" and force System 2 to engage.**

---

### The Two Systems

| System 1 (Fast) | System 2 (Slow) |
|-----------------|-----------------|
| Automatic, always on | Deliberate, effortful |
| Effortless | Lazy, limited capacity |
| Generates intuitions | Endorses or overrides |
| "What you see is all there is" (WYSIATI) | Can check for missing info |
| Prone to biases | Can apply rules and algorithms |
| Substitutes easy questions for hard ones | Can solve the actual question |
| Can't be turned off | Depletes with use |

**Trading Implication:** My forecasts START in System 1. I must deliberately engage System 2 to check biases — but System 2 is lazy and will endorse System 1's answer if not forced to work. Checklists and algorithms force System 2 engagement.

---

### Part I: The Heuristics and Biases

**1. Anchoring (Chapter 11)**
- ANY number you see anchors your estimate
- Dice roll + sentencing: judges anchored on random dice!
- Even absurd anchors move estimates (Gandhi's age > 9 years old?)
- **Trading Rule:** Generate my estimate BEFORE checking market price. Market price is an anchor.

**2. Availability Heuristic (Chapter 12-13)**
- We judge frequency by ease of retrieval
- Media coverage ≠ actual frequency
- Availability cascades: minor events + media = public panic → policy overreaction
- **Trading Rule:** Ask "Am I estimating probability or recency of memory?"

**3. Representativeness & Base Rate Neglect (Chapters 14-16)**
- Tom W problem: PhD students ignored base rates, judged by stereotype
- Linda the feminist bank teller: conjunction fallacy (A∩B > B is impossible!)
- "Substitution was perfect: participants did nothing but judge representativeness"
- Causal base rates ARE used; statistical base rates are ignored
- **Trading Rule:** Start EVERY forecast from base rates. Write it down as anchor.

**4. Regression to Mean (Chapter 17)**
- Flight instructors: punishment "worked" because regression followed extremes
- Sports Illustrated jinx = regression, not causation
- **Trading Rule:** Expect extreme performance to regress. Don't over-update on outliers.

**5. Intuitive Predictions (Chapter 18)**
- "Julie reads fluently at 4" → estimate GPA? We predict by representativeness, not regression
- Predictions should be LESS extreme than evidence suggests
- **Trading Rule:** Regress predictions toward the base rate. More extreme evidence → less regression, but always some.

---

### Part III: Overconfidence

**6. Illusion of Understanding (Narrative Fallacy) (Chapter 19)**
- "Google succeeded because..." — we construct coherent stories post-hoc
- Hindsight bias: "I knew it all along" distorts memory of past beliefs
- **Trading Rule:** Record predictions BEFORE outcomes. Compare to see if I "knew it all along."

**7. Illusion of Validity (Chapter 20)**
- Israeli military interviews: felt confident, had near-zero validity
- "Subjective confidence is a feeling, not a judgment"
- Coherent story → confidence, regardless of evidence quality
- Stock pickers: 0.01 correlation between rankings year-to-year = pure luck
- **Trading Rule:** Coherence ≠ accuracy. My confidence tells me about my story, not reality.

**8. Clinical vs Statistical Prediction (Chapter 21)**
- Meehl: formulas beat experts in ~60% of studies, tied in rest
- "Human decision makers are inferior to a prediction formula even when given the formula's score"
- Simple equally-weighted formulas often beat complex regression
- **Trading Rule:** Use checklists and formulas. Override them only with "broken leg" evidence.

**9. Expert Intuition (Chapter 22)**
- When can you trust intuition? (1) Regular environment, (2) Prolonged practice, (3) Fast feedback
- Firefighters, chess masters: valid intuition because environment is learnable
- Stock pickers, political pundits: zero-validity environment → intuition is worthless
- **Trading Rule:** Trust pattern recognition only in domains with clear feedback loops.

**10. Outside View & Planning Fallacy (Chapter 23)**
- Kahneman's curriculum: team estimated 2 years, actual was 8 years
- Seymour knew base rate (7+ years, 40% failure) but still said 2 years
- "Irrational perseverance": after learning outside view, they continued anyway!
- Reference class forecasting: the cure
- **Trading Rule:** ALWAYS ask "What happened in similar cases?" before estimating.

**11. Optimistic Bias (Chapter 24)**
- Entrepreneurs: 81% believed their chance of success was 70%+; actual is ~35%
- Competition neglect: "We have a good product" without asking "What are competitors doing?"
- Premortem: "Imagine we failed. Why?" Overcomes groupthink.
- **Trading Rule:** Run a premortem before every significant trade.

### Part IV: Choices (Prospect Theory)

**12. Bernoulli's Error & Reference Dependence (Chapter 25)**
- Utility theory assumes outcomes = states of wealth
- WRONG: outcomes = CHANGES from reference point
- Jack and Jill: same wealth, different happiness because different histories
- **Trading Rule:** Track how I frame outcomes. Am I anchoring on purchase price?

**13. Loss Aversion (Chapters 26-29)**
- Losses hurt ~2× more than equivalent gains feel good
- Loss aversion ratio: 1.5 to 2.5 (need $200 gain to offset $100 loss risk)
- Brain imaging: amygdala responds to threatening images even if consciously unseen
- "Bad is stronger than good" across all domains
- **Trading Rule:** My loss aversion distorts judgment. Ask "Would I enter this position now at current price?"

**14. Endowment Effect (Chapter 27)**
- Owning something increases its value to you
- Mug experiment: sellers demanded $7.12, buyers offered $2.87
- Experienced traders don't show endowment effect ("think like a trader")
- **Trading Rule:** If I wouldn't buy at current price, I should consider selling.

**15. Risk-Seeking in Losses (Chapter 29)**
- "The sure loss is very aversive, and this drives you to take the risk"
- Top-right of fourfold pattern: desperate gambles to avoid certain loss
- "Businesses that are losing ground often waste assets in futile attempts to catch up"
- **Trading Rule:** When all options are bad, I'll be risk-seeking. Force System 2 to evaluate.

**16. The Fourfold Pattern (Chapter 29)**
```
                    HIGH PROBABILITY    LOW PROBABILITY
GAINS               Risk-averse         Risk-seeking
(95% win $10K)      (Accept $9K sure)   (Buy lottery)
                    
LOSSES              Risk-seeking        Risk-averse

```
- **Trading Rule:** Know which cell I'm in. Each has characteristic errors.

**17. Rare Events (Chapter 30)**
- Vivid imagery overwhelms probability assessment
- Denominator neglect: "1 in 1,000" scarier than "0.1%"
- Terrorists understand this: maximize availability, not actual harm
- **Trading Rule:** Force myself to state probabilities numerically, not narratively.

**18. Sunk Cost Fallacy & Mental Accounting (Chapter 32)**
- Driving into blizzard because tickets were pre-paid
- Disposition effect: sell winners, hold losers (wrong!)
- "If you care about wealth, sell the loser Tiffany, keep the winner Blueberry"
- **Trading Rule:** Sunk costs are sunk. Ask "What should I do NOW?"

**19. Regret & Taboo Tradeoffs (Chapter 32)**
- Commission vs omission: action that fails evokes more regret than inaction
- Anticipation of regret drives risk aversion
- **Trading Rule:** "Would I regret NOT taking this trade?" Balance both regrets.

**20. Framing Effects (Chapter 34)**
- "90% survival rate" vs "10% mortality" → same fact, different choices
- Opt-out organ donation: 100% donors; Opt-in: 4% donors
- Physicians affected by framing just like laypeople
- **Trading Rule:** Reframe every decision both ways. Different frames → different intuitions.

### Part V: Two Selves

**21. Experiencing Self vs Remembering Self (Chapter 35-38)**
- Duration neglect: total pain doesn't matter, only peak and end
- Peak-end rule: colonoscopy study — shorter procedure rated worse if ended at peak
- Cold-hand experiment: 90 seconds preferred over 60 if ended mildly
- **Trading Rule:** My memory of trades is distorted. Log actual outcomes, not remembered feelings.

**22. Focusing Illusion (Chapter 38)**
- "Nothing in life is as important as you think it is when you are thinking about it"
- Paraplegics: bad mood ~50% of time initially, regress toward normal
- Miswanting: predict wrong about what will make us happy
- Income satiation: ~$75K; beyond that, life satisfaction rises but experienced happiness doesn't
- **Trading Rule:** Don't over-weight any single trade's importance. Long-term track record matters.

## The Signal and the Noise — Nate Silver
**Status:** ✅ COMPLETE (read cover to cover 2026-03-18)

### Core Philosophy
The book spans 13 domains (finance, politics, sports, weather, earthquakes, economics, disease, gambling, chess, poker, climate, terrorism) to extract universal truths about prediction. Central thesis: **The signal is the truth. The noise is what distracts us from it.** Our predictions fail when we mistake noise for signal, or when we lack the humility to acknowledge how much we don't know.

---

### The Three Laws of Prediction

**1. Predictability varies by domain**
- Some fields (weather, baseball) show steady improvement with better data/methods
- Some fields (earthquakes, economics) resist prediction despite massive effort
- Know which domain you're in

**2. Foxes beat hedgehogs**
- Hedgehogs know "one big thing" — single grand theory
- Foxes know "many things" — synthesize multiple approaches
- In forecasting: foxes win, hedgehogs make memorable predictions that fail

**3. Overconfidence is the cardinal sin**
- Across all domains, forecasters are too confident
- The worse the track record, the more confident the forecaster
- Acknowledge uncertainty or pay the price

---

### Part I: Baseball & Weather (Chapters 1-4)

**1. Out-of-Sample Failure**
- Financial models worked "in sample" but collapsed in 2008
- Rating agencies: extrapolated from housing data without downturn
- **Trading Rule:** Ask "Has this thesis been tested out-of-sample?" If only tested in calm conditions, discount edge.

**2. The Human-Computer Partnership**
- Pure statistical models plateau; adding human judgment extends improvement
- Weather forecasting: best results when humans adjust computer output
- **Trading Rule:** Don't trust raw models. Add human judgment, especially for regime changes.

**3. Data Rich ≠ Signal Rich**
- More data often means more noise to drown signal
- Baseball stats exploded; predictive gains were modest
- **Trading Rule:** Don't be seduced by data volume. Signal-to-noise ratio matters more.

**4. "The map is not the territory"**
- Economic models are simplifications, not reality
- When map conflicts with territory, territory wins
- **Trading Rule:** Models are approximations. Reality breaks models at extremes.

---

### Part II: Earthquakes & Economics (Chapters 5-6)

**5. Overfitting: The False Positive Trap**
- Seismologists found "patterns" that failed to predict anything
- Too many variables, not enough earthquakes = spurious correlations
- **Trading Rule:** Be deeply skeptical of patterns discovered through data mining. Ask: "What theory generated this prediction?"

**6. Causal Understanding Beats Pattern-Matching**
- We know earthquakes WILL happen (plate tectonics)
- We can't predict WHEN (too complex, chaotic)
- Prediction = causal theory + reliable data
- **Trading Rule:** Without causal mechanism, correlation is probably noise.

**7. Economic Forecasters Have Negative Value**
- Consensus forecasts miss recessions entirely
- Adding all their predictions DECREASES accuracy vs. simple benchmarks
- **Trading Rule:** Economic consensus is for anchoring only. Don't trust the consensus to call turns.

**8. The Keynesian Beauty Contest**
- Markets price "what others think others think"
- Fundamentals matter less than crowd psychology short-term
- **Trading Rule:** Short-term moves are noise. Focus on where fundamentals win: longer horizons.

---

### Part III: Disease, Poker, Gambling (Chapters 7-10)

**9. Initial Conditions Are Everything**
- Flu forecasts swing wildly based on early-outbreak assumptions
- Small changes in R₀ → huge changes in predictions
- **Trading Rule:** Identify which inputs drive my forecast most. Focus validation there.

**10. Bayesian Thinking Is Correct**
- Thomas Bayes + frequentist debate: Bayes wins for decision-making
- Update beliefs incrementally with new evidence
- **Trading Rule:** Every forecast is a prior to be updated, not a conclusion to defend.

**11. Garbage In, Garbage Out**
- HIV models were wrong because input data was wrong
- Even perfect models fail with bad inputs
- **Trading Rule:** Validate inputs before trusting model outputs.

**12. Process Over Outcomes (Poker's Lesson)**
- Good decision + bad luck = bad outcome. Still a good decision.
- "Results-oriented thinking" is the enemy
- Play well and win; play well and lose; play badly and lose; play badly and win — only process distinguishes skill
- **Trading Rule:** Judge my decisions by the process, not the P&L. Log reasoning, review even wins.

**13. Tilt Is the Enemy**
- Emotional reaction to variance destroys edge
- Entitlement ("I deserved that pot") triggers tilt
- **Trading Rule:** Recognize emotional state before trading. If tilted: STOP.

---

### Part IV: Efficient Markets (Chapter 11)

**14. No Free Lunch (Mostly True)**
- Mutual funds don't beat market consistently
- High-fee funds especially don't
- Transaction costs eat whatever edge you think you have
- **Trading Rule:** Every trade has transaction costs. Need real edge, not theoretical edge.

**15. Chartists Are Noise Traders**
- Technical analysis = mistaking noise for signal
- Random walks look like patterns; patterns look like random walks
- **Trading Rule:** Don't trust price charts. Trade on information about the underlying event.

**16. Aggregation Beats Individuals**
- Average of all forecasts beats most individuals
- Even best individuals rarely beat average consistently
- **Trading Rule:** Market price IS aggregated wisdom. My edge must come from something market doesn't know, not from being "smarter."

**17. Short-Term Focus Creates Long-Term Mispricings**
- Traders judged on 90-day returns → ignore fundamentals beyond that
- Herding is rational for career survival
- **Trading Rule:** When I have longer horizon than marginal trader, I may have edge in fundamentals.

**18. Bubbles Happen Because of Rational Herding**
- Individual rationality → collective irrationality
- Everyone knows bubble will pop; no one wants to be early
- **Trading Rule:** Bubbles are identifiable but not tradeable short-term. P/E extremes predict long-term, not next quarter.

**19. Overconfidence Drives Excess Trading**
- Trading volumes far exceed rational rebalancing
- Every trade = two people disagreeing (both can't be right)
- **Trading Rule:** If I'm trading frequently, I'm probably overconfident. Slow down.

**20. "Buy High, Sell Low" Is Human Default**
- Surveys show investors bullish at tops, bearish at bottoms
- 67% thought stocks were good buy at 2000 peak; 26% at 1990 bottom
- **Trading Rule:** My gut feeling about market direction is probably wrong at extremes.

---

### Part V: Climate & Terrorism (Chapters 12-13)

**21. Three Types of Uncertainty**
- Initial condition: short-term noise (weather within climate)
- Scenario: policy/economic assumptions 
- Structural: model inadequacy
- **Trading Rule:** For each prediction, identify which uncertainty dominates. Price that into confidence.

**22. Simple Models Sometimes Beat Complex Ones**
- Climate: simple CO2→temperature model matched actuals better than complex IPCC
- Armstrong's criticism: complexity ≠ accuracy
- **Trading Rule:** Prefer simple models with strong causal theory over complex data-fit models.

**23. Unknown Unknowns (Pearl Harbor, 9/11)**
- "We confuse the unfamiliar with the improbable"
- Signals present but not interpreted because hypothesis never considered
- **Trading Rule:** For rare events, ask "What am I NOT imagining?"

**24. Power Laws and Fat Tails**
- Terror attacks follow power law: many small, few huge
- September 11 was NOT an outlier statistically — it was expected from the distribution
- **Trading Rule:** For tail-risk events, don't use normal distribution. Ask: "What does power law predict?"

**25. Think Big About Low-Probability/High-Impact Events**
- Most terrorism risk comes from rare mega-attacks, not frequent small ones
- Israel's strategy: tolerate small attacks, prevent large ones
- **Trading Rule:** Focus analytical effort on high-impact events, even if low-probability.

1. **Think probabilistically** — Express beliefs as percentages, not certainties
2. **Know where you're coming from** — State priors explicitly before examining evidence
3. **Try and err** — Make lots of forecasts, track results, iterate
4. **Aggregate wisely** — Weight multiple models, multiple views
5. **Be a fox** — Synthesize, don't crusade for one theory
6. **Acknowledge uncertainty** — Wider intervals are honest, not weak

### 🦊 FOX VS HEDGEHOG CHECK

**HEDGEHOG (Dangerous):**
- One grand theory explains everything
- Dismisses contradicting evidence
- Very confident in predictions
- "This time is different" or "It's obvious"
- Poor track record, doesn't self-correct

**FOX (Correct):**
- Multiple theories, weighted by evidence
- Actively seeks disconfirming info
- Calibrated confidence (uncertain when should be)
- "It depends" and "On the other hand"
- Updates beliefs with new data

**Am I Being a Hedgehog?**
- [ ] Do I have ONE dominant theory for this market?
- [ ] Am I dismissing evidence against my view?
- [ ] Am I more confident than the situation warrants?
- [ ] Would I be embarrassed to be wrong?

**If any YES → engage fox mode**

### 📊 DOMAIN CLASSIFICATION

**HIGH PREDICTABILITY:**
- Fast feedback, lots of data
- Examples: Weather (short-term), baseball stats, election polling
- Approach: Trust models, smaller edge needed

**MEDIUM PREDICTABILITY:**
- Recurring events, moderate noise
- Examples: Economic releases, quarterly earnings
- Approach: Models + human judgment, medium edge needed

**LOW PREDICTABILITY:**
- Rare events, long horizons, complex systems
- Examples: Earthquakes, political shifts, regulatory changes
- Approach: Wide confidence intervals, need large edge to trade

### ⚠️ OVERCONFIDENCE CHECKPOINTS

**Symptom** → **Response**

"I'm sure this will happen" → State as probability. What would change your mind?

"The market is wrong" → Why are smart people on the other side?

"This time is different" → What's the base rate? Why believe exception?

"I knew it all along" → Did you record prediction BEFORE outcome?

"My thesis is elegant" → Complexity penalty. Simple ≠ correct.

**Calibration Rule:**
When you say 80% confident → you should be right 80% of time
Track and adjust if systematically off

### 🎯 PROCESS SCORE PROTOCOL

**After EVERY trade resolution:**

| Dimension | Score | Criteria |
|-----------|-------|----------|
| Outcome | +1 / 0 / -1 | Profit / Scratch / Loss |
| Process | +1 / 0 / -1 | Followed rules / Minor deviation / Broke rules |

**The Insight:**
- High Outcome Score + Low Process Score = Got lucky, will regress
- Low Outcome Score + High Process Score = Got unlucky, will recover
- Process Score predicts future success; Outcome Score doesn't

**Monthly Review:**
- Process Score < 0.8? → Review rules, identify weak points
- Process Score > 0.8 but Outcome Score low? → Variance, stay the course
- Both scores low? → Fundamental strategy problem

### 🔄 THE BAYESIAN UPDATE LOOP

```
1. PRIOR: Start with market price as best estimate
          (Market knows things I don't)

2. EVIDENCE: What new information do I have?
             (Must be genuine, not reframed old info)

3. LIKELIHOOD: How much does this change probability?
               (Be honest — usually less than I think)

4. POSTERIOR: Update estimate proportionally
              (Weighted average of prior + new evidence)

5. ACTION: If edge > threshold → Trade
           If edge < threshold → Monitor
           If uncertain → Wait for more evidence
```

**Key Rule:** Small evidence → small updates. Don't swing beliefs wildly.
