### Part II Complete (Chapters 8-9)

**Chapter 8 "Why People Trade":**
- Trading is ZERO-SUM — for you to profit, someone must lose
- Trader taxonomy: Utilitarian (investors, hedgers, gamblers) | Profit-motivated (speculators, dealers) | Futile (pseudo-informed)
- Investment-motivated trading = only ~1% of volume!

**Chapter 9 "Good Markets":**
- Markets aggregate information into prices (better than any individual)
- Command economies fail because they can't aggregate info like markets
- Efficient markets → random walk prices
- Informative prices are critical for capital allocation

### Part III Complete (Chapters 10-12) — "Speculators"

**Chapter 10 "Informed Traders and Market Efficiency":**
- 4 types: Value traders, News traders, Technical traders, Arbitrageurs
- Precision + orthogonality = profitability (right when others are wrong)
- Pseudo-informed = trading on STALE info (most common mistake)
- Markets efficient = prices reflect what can be PROFITABLY traded upon

**Chapter 11 "Order Anticipators":**
- Front runners: trade ahead of your orders (protect info!)
- Quote matchers: extract option value from limit orders
- Sentiment traders: predict uninformed trades (momentum vulnerable)
- Squeezers: corner markets (always have exit paths)

**Chapter 12 "Bluffers and Manipulation":**
- Bluffers profit from asymmetric buy/sell impacts
- Momentum traders = #1 victim ("why else would it go up?")
- Value traders can call bluffs — but must KNOW the value

### Part IV Complete (Chapters 13-18) — "Liquidity Suppliers"

**Chapter 13 "Dealers":**
- Dealers profit by buying at bid, selling at ask — but realized spread < quoted spread
- Adverse selection is THE key determinant of dealer profitability
- Order flow reveals information; one-sided flow = informed trading
- If you're providing liquidity via limit orders, you face dealer risks

**Chapter 14 "Bid/Ask Spreads":**
- **MOST IMPORTANT LESSON**: Uninformed traders lose REGARDLESS of order type
- Market orders = pay adverse selection spread; Limit orders = suffer direct adverse selection
- The ONLY way to avoid losing to informed traders is to NOT TRADE
- Spread width determines optimal order choice (wide → limits; narrow → market)

**Chapter 15 "Block Traders":**
- Large orders face 4 problems: latent demand, exposure, price discrimination, asymmetric info
- ~80% of blocks are seller-initiated (sellers more credible)
- Sunshine trading rarely works — attracts front runners

**Chapter 16 "Value Traders":**
- Value traders = ultimate liquidity suppliers (trade when price ≠ value)
- Winner's curse: if you win the auction, you probably overbid
- Value traders must be BEST informed to avoid getting picked off by news traders

**Chapter 17 "Arbitrageurs":**
- Pure arbitrage = forced convergence (delivery mechanism ensures reversion)
- Speculative arbitrage = basis may NEVER close
- **LTCM lesson**: They were RIGHT but went bankrupt. Never leverage to the max — leave staying power!
- Four risks: implementation, basis, model, carrying cost

**Chapter 18 "Buy-Side Traders":**
- Order exposure is a tradeoff (find counterparties vs attract front runners)
- Impatient → market orders; Patient → limit orders
- Proactive traders search; reactive traders wait for opportunities

### Part V Complete (Chapters 19-20) — "Origins of Liquidity and Volatility"

**Chapter 19 "Liquidity":**
- Liquidity = ability to trade large size, quickly, at low cost
- Three dimensions: DEPTH (size), IMMEDIACY (speed), WIDTH (cost)
- Liquidity is bilateral search — impatient demand it, patient supply it
- Five supplier types: market makers, block dealers, value traders, precommitted, arbitrageurs
- Market resiliency = how quickly prices revert after uninformed trading

**Chapter 20 "Volatility":**
- Two types: FUNDAMENTAL (random walk, unpredictable) and TRANSITORY (reverting, = transaction costs)
- Transitory vol identified by negative serial correlation (reversals)
- High-storage-cost commodities are volatile (can't buffer supply/demand mismatches)
- High P/E stocks more volatile than low P/E (growth uncertainty)
- Regulators can affect transitory volatility but NOT fundamental volatility

### Part VI Complete (Chapters 21-22) — "Evaluation and Prediction"

**Chapter 21 "Transaction Cost Measurement":**
- Transaction costs = explicit + implicit + missed opportunity
- Implementation shortfall = BEST metric (decision-time benchmark, ungameable)
- VWAP is gameable — brokers can spread trades to match
- Split order bias: large orders look cheaper because benchmark moves with fills
- BALANCE costs vs missed opportunities — optimize aggressiveness

**Chapter 22 "Performance Evaluation and Prediction":**
- ⚠️ **PAST PERFORMANCE DOES NOT PREDICT FUTURE** — this is MATH, not opinion
- Need >20 YEARS of data to identify 2% alpha with 95% confidence
- Sample selection bias: you only hear about winners (losers disappear)
- **Peso problem**: many small wins + rare huge losses LOOKS like skill
- **Regression to the mean**: extreme performers revert (Buffet's first 26 yrs were partly luck)
- **COMPARATIVE ADVANTAGE wins** — not "am I good?" but "am I BETTER?"

### Part VII Complete (Chapters 23-29) — "Market Structure"

**Chapter 23 "Index and Portfolio Markets":**
- Index markets more liquid than underlying (few informed about broad values)
- Active managers MUST underperform on average (zero-sum + costs = negative-sum)
- Russell reconstitution creates predictable effects: adds +15% June, -5% July reversal
- Portfolio trading has less adverse selection than single-security trading

**Chapter 24 "Specialists":**
- Price continuity is PUBLIC GOOD competitive markets won't provide
- Cream-skimming: specialists step in front of profitable limit orders
- Stopped stock = look-back timing option (valuable to specialists)

**Chapter 25 "Internalization, Preferencing, and Crossing":**
- Retail can audit commissions easily, execution quality NOT easily
- Internalization/preferencing shifts power from limit orders to dealers

**Chapter 26 "Competition Within and Among Markets":**
- Order flow externality: liquidity attracts liquidity (natural consolidation)
- Time precedence NOT enforced across market segments
- Arbitrageurs consolidate fragmented markets by moving liquidity

**Chapter 27 "Floor vs Automated Trading Systems":**
- Floor excels when traders need info exchange before committing
- Electronic provides better audit trails (flawless record keeping)

**Chapter 28 "Bubbles, Crashes, and Circuit Breakers":**
- Most crashes = CORRECTIONS to previous pricing errors (not failures)
- Portfolio insurance/stop-losses DESTABILIZE when widely used
- Trading halts change discriminatory → uniform pricing (protects suppliers)
- Gravitational effect: circuit breakers can INCREASE volatility
- Traders PANIC when uncertain about RISK (not just direction)

**Chapter 29 "Insider Trading":**
- Insider trading INCREASES bid/ask spreads (more adverse selection)
- Competition among insiders reveals information quickly
- Monopolist insiders trade slowly; competing insiders trade fast
