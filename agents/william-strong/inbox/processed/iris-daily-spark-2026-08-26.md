## The Spark: publish the loss record

Chris already believes in this idea — in a different room.

Nolan, Calvin, and Elliot all publish graded results. Not just the winners: the number, the line, the date, and whether it hit. Every serious bettor Chris follows does this, and the reason is structural — **a capper with no published loss record is assumed to be lying.** Not "less trusted." *Assumed to be lying.* You cannot evaluate a record you can't see, so an all-wins feed reads as a marketing feed, and everyone discounts it to zero.

Now look at real estate, where the entire industry does the opposite. No agent has ever posted "the number I gave you in July was wrong." They believe the correction costs them the listing.

Here's the inversion, and it's specific to exactly where Chris is standing right now:

**A public correction is the only credential that proves you were here last week.**

Chris has no NH track record, no sold signs, no referral chain, no ten-years-in-town. Those all require time and cannot be bought or faked. But a correction requires a *prior published position* — which means it is documentary evidence of continuity. It says *I was making calls in this market before today, in public, under my name, and I stayed to answer for them.* A brand-new agent cannot manufacture a sold sign. He can absolutely manufacture proof that he was paying attention in July, because he was.

That is a credential the incumbents have and never use, sitting on the ground, free.

And note what Jack already does instinctively — the newsletter opens with *"Last week I called the single 2-point dip 'the first crack' in a five-week climb. It held."* He references his own prior call. The move is already in his voice. It only gets deployed when the prior call turned out **right**. The whole idea here is: run the identical sentence when it turned out wrong.

## Three rules that make it work, and one that will kill it

**1. Don't brand it.** No series name, no swoosh, no cobalt/sunflower card, no logo lockup. Type-only or plain text. The credibility comes entirely from it looking *unformatted* — like an interruption to the content calendar rather than an entry in it. The moment a correction gets a designed template, it reads as a stunt and it's worth less than nothing. This is the rare case where our brand system should stay in its box.

**2. Trigger-based, never calendar-based.** Run one when there is one. Never schedule one. If two months pass with nothing to correct, that's a good two months — post nothing. The instant this becomes a weekly slot, we start manufacturing errors to fill it, which is precisely the disease I diagnosed on Aug 24 with the urgency monoculture: a format that *must* fire every week will find a reason, and by Wednesday the reasons are fake.

**3. Correct the number, never the person.** "We had it wrong," never "Fiona had it wrong." One voice, one owner, no internal laundry in public.

**4. The thing that kills it:** apologizing. No "we sincerely regret," no "we strive for accuracy," no throat-clearing. State the wrong number, state the right number, state where the right one came from, move on. Any sentence that sounds like a press release converts the asset back into a liability. Jack's *"and I'm not going to spin it"* is the exact register — flat, unbothered, done in six words.
