Philadelphia gets the credit. But New Hampshire was first.

On January 5, 1776 — six months before John Hancock's bold signature dried on parchment in Philadelphia — a group of delegates gathered in the small river town of Exeter, New Hampshire, and did something no colonial body had yet done: they voted to govern themselves without the King.

Not provisionally. Not symbolically. Formally, by vote, with a written constitution.

The Continental Congress wouldn't get there until July. New Hampshire got there in January, in the middle of winter, in a colony that most people today couldn't place on a map.

---

To understand why, you have to understand how completely the old order had collapsed in New Hampshire by late 1775.

Royal Governor John Wentworth — the last of his line, a man who had genuinely tried to be a reasonable governor — had been driven out. The previous summer, after a colonial militia seized the fort at Portsmouth harbor, Wentworth and his family boarded a British warship in the harbor and never came back. There was no functioning royal government. No courts. No official structure. Just the Provincial Congress, meeting in ad hoc sessions, trying to hold together a colony that was technically still part of the British Empire but practically operating on its own.

The Continental Congress, watching this chaos from Philadelphia, sent New Hampshire an unusual message: sort yourselves out. "We recommend," they wrote, that New Hampshire establish "such a form of government as in their judgment will best produce the happiness of the people."

It was a permission slip from a body that technically had no authority to issue one. But it was enough.

The Fifth New Hampshire Provincial Congress convened in Exeter — a town of about 1,400 people, recently elevated to de facto capital because Portsmouth was too close to the British warships in the harbor. On January 5, 1776, they voted to adopt a temporary constitution.

It was a modest document — barely 500 words — and they were at pains to call it temporary, fully expecting that the dispute with Britain would eventually resolve and normal royal government would resume. "We never designed to declare an independence from Great Britain," one delegate explained. That wasn't what this was about, they insisted.

But it was. They just didn't know it yet.

---

The Exeter constitution was, in practice, the founding act of American republican self-government. It abolished royal authority, established an elected assembly and a council, and created the framework for a functioning independent state — weeks before Thomas Paine's *Common Sense* hit the streets and months before Jefferson sat down to write his famous draft.

Meshech Weare, a farmer-lawyer from Hampton Falls, became the president of the new council — the closest thing New Hampshire had to a governor, though no one wanted to use that word yet. He was not a glamorous figure. He dressed simply, spoke plainly, and distrusted anyone who enjoyed power too much. He would serve New Hampshire through the entire Revolutionary period, a steady hand in a colony that needed one desperately.

Matthew Thornton, an Irish-born physician who had practiced in Londonderry, was a delegate to that January session. He would later travel to Philadelphia and add his signature to the Declaration of Independence — one of three New Hampshire men to sign — but his earlier, quieter act of governance in Exeter was arguably more radical. Declaring independence is easy. Actually building a government from nothing, in January, in a small town in the middle of a colony that wasn't sure it wanted to be a republic — that takes something else.

---

Here is the part that doesn't make it into the history textbooks: the New Hampshire constitution of January 1776 wasn't a triumphant moment. It was a desperate one.

The men in Exeter were worried. The war was going badly — the assault on Quebec had failed, British ships were in the harbor, and no one knew how long the standoff would last. They weren't writing a founding document out of idealism. They were solving an immediate problem: how do you run a colony when the entire legal framework that ran it has walked out the door?

They solved it the New Hampshire way: quietly, practically, without much ceremony, getting the job done before anyone in Philadelphia had worked up the nerve to suggest it.

"Live Free or Die" came later — a toast by General John Stark in 1809, thirty years after the Revolution. But the attitude it describes was already present in Exeter in January 1776. Not as a slogan. As a fact.

---

Every July 4th, Americans celebrate the Declaration of Independence — the document that announced to the world what the colonies were doing. And it deserves the celebration. Jefferson's prose still rings. The signatures still matter.

But somewhere in the chain of events that produced that document, there was a winter morning in a small New Hampshire town where a group of farmers and lawyers and physicians sat down in a draughty room and did the unglamorous work of actually making it real.

Philadelphia gets the credit.

But New Hampshire was first.

---

*Today's footnote: The Exeter constitution was so "temporary" that New Hampshire didn't replace it with a permanent one until 1784 — by which point the Revolution had been won and the temporary fix had lasted nearly a decade.*
