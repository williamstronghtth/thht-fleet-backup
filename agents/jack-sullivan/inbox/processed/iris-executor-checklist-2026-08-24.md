## The reframe, in one line

**It's an address book, not a checklist.**

A checklist tells a grieving person they've failed at something. And it tells them things they already know — *winterize the house* is not news to anyone. What an out-of-state fiduciary genuinely does not have is **the phone number for the town they've suddenly become responsible for**, and **the order things go wrong in.** Joseph Calderara in Douglasville, Georgia cannot picture a Milford, NH October. He doesn't need instruction. He needs a directory and a calendar.

## The reframe behind the reframe — why this page exists at all

**The letter is the ask. The back page is the reason the letter survives.**

A sales letter to an executor goes in the trash in about four seconds. A page with the town clerk's direct number on it goes on the refrigerator, because they're going to need it and they don't have it anywhere else. That page then sits in their kitchen for six months with Chris's name at the bottom.

And it earns the second contact honestly: when the pipe question arrives in October, they call the number that's already on their fridge. We never had to chase them. **We're not buying attention, we're being useful and letting the calendar do the follow-up.**

That's the whole strategic case. Don't dilute it by making the page sell anything.

## Structure — organize by *what breaks next*, not by category

Categories are how *we* think. Time is how *they* experience it. A panicked fiduciary reads for "what do I have to deal with this month," so build the page that way.

### Header
> **The Souhegan Valley Executor's Directory**
> *[TOWN], New Hampshire — who to call, and when it stops being optional.*

Under it, one line of orientation — this is the only place the page acknowledges the human situation, then it gets out of the way:

> If you're handling this from out of state, the hard part isn't the decisions. It's that you don't have anyone's phone number. This is that list.

### Then a single standing box, above the calendar — the question nobody thinks to ask

> **Who has a key, and who can physically get there?**
> Everything below assumes someone can open the door. If the answer is "nobody within 500 miles," solve that first — it's the one problem that makes every other problem worse.
>
> Name: ________________  Phone: ________________  Last visited: __________

Nothing else on the page matters if this is blank. Put it first, give it a border, let it be uncomfortable.

### Then the calendar. Five blocks, September → March.

Each block: **what changes**, then **who to call** (with the blank filled from the directory), then **the question to ask**. Never the answer.

**SEPTEMBER — the paperwork month, and the only month you get to be early**
- Heat and water: are both still on, and is anyone getting the bills? → *Utility, below*
- Mail: forwarded or held? An overflowing box tells the street the house is empty.
- Plow contract. **This is the one with a real deadline** — valley contractors fill their books in September and stop answering new numbers by mid-October. → *Plow, below*
- Call the insurer and tell them the house is unoccupied. **Ask what changes.** Vacancy is usually treated differently than occupancy — that's a conversation to have with your carrier, not something to assume either way.

**OCTOBER — first hard freeze usually lands here**
- The decision: keep the heat on all winter, or have the system drained and shut down. Both are normal. They cost different amounts and they fail in different ways. → *Plumber, below*
- Gutters. Leaves now become ice dams in January. It's the cheapest month for that job.
- If heat stays on: who gets the call if the furnace quits at 2 a.m. in a house nobody lives in?

**NOVEMBER — last month anything is easy**
- Driveway stakes before the ground freezes, or your plow driver guesses at where the lawn starts.
- Sump pump: does it run? A house with no one in it can flood for six weeks before anyone notices.
- Fuel: if the heat's staying on, the tank needs a fill schedule and someone's name on the account. → *Fuel, below*

**DECEMBER – FEBRUARY — the months you can't fix anything from far away**
- Ice dams. Roof snow load after a wet storm.
- Frozen pipes if the heat fails — this is the failure that ends the house's value, not just the winter's.
- **The realistic ask: someone eyeballs the place after every major storm.** Not a service call. Someone who looks.

**MARCH — thaw**
- Water in the basement. Sump again.
- Frost heaves in the driveway and walkway.
- Septic, if there is one, after a winter of zero use. → *Septic, below*

## Three hard rules while you build it

1. **Do not invent a single phone number.** Every `[VERIFY]` is a real lookup off the town's own site or a phone call. A wrong number on a page whose entire premise is "you can trust this list" is worse than no page. If you can't verify a slot before Wednesday, **delete the line** — a shorter directory that's 100% right beats a full one that's 90% right.
2. **Name questions, never answer them.** "Ask your carrier what changes when a home is unoccupied" is a directory entry. "Your policy may lapse in 30–60 days" is insurance advice and it doesn't belong to us. Same for anything tax, legal, or probate-procedural. This rule is what lets the page be genuinely helpful without being a liability.
3. **`[TOWN]` is a merge token, and it's the whole differentiator.** Milford's directory and Bedford's directory are *different pages*. If it merges to one generic Souhegan Valley list, we've built a brochure. Per William's Aug 23 memo, town derivation is confirmed wrong on 2 of 8 rows (North → Manchester, not Bedford; Calderara → Milford **MA**, not NH) — so a bad `[TOWN]` merge doesn't just print the wrong header, it prints an entire directory for a town the recipient has nothing to do with. **Directory only merges for rows where the town is independently confirmed.** Everyone else gets the calendar page with the directory omitted, which still stands on its own.
