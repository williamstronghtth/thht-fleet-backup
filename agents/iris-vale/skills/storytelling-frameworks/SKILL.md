# Storytelling Frameworks

Facts inform; stories move. The same information arranged as a list gets skimmed and forgotten, while arranged as a story gets watched to the end and remembered. This skill is a kit of proven structures and the judgment of which to use when.

## Pick the structure to fit the job

**Before–After–Bridge (BAB).** Best for: ads, emails, short video, any persuasion. Paint the current pain (Before), show the better world (After), position the offer as the Bridge between them. Fast and reliable. *Use for Jack's outreach, Derek's roofing, quick promos.*

**StoryBrand — customer as hero.** Best for: brand pages, longer pitches, "why us." The customer is the hero, not the business; the business is the guide who helps them win. They have a problem, you give them a plan, you call them to action, and you stake the success and the failure. *Use for THHT's positioning, agent bios, relocation content — especially the NH market where it's all about earning trust.*

**Hook → Tension → Payoff.** Best for: history, social, anything that lives or dies on curiosity. Open on the surprising thing, build a small question or stakes, then resolve it satisfyingly. *This is Arthur's native beat — borrow it for Fiona's "On This Street" series and for video.*

**Three-act listing tour.** Best for: listing descriptions and property videos. Act 1 — the arrival/first impression and who this home is *for*. Act 2 — the journey through the spaces, leading with the one or two genuinely distinctive features (the "edge"). Act 3 — the life lived here (the Saturday morning, the holidays, the view) and the call to act. Sensory and specific throughout, never a feature checklist.

**The stakes frame.** Best for: betting and markets writeups (Nolan, Calvin, Elliot, Oliver), and any "why this matters now." Don't bury the conclusion in analysis. Lead with the call and the conviction, name what's at stake, *then* show the reasoning. People follow confidence backed by logic, not logic searching for a conclusion.

**In medias res.** Best for: video and email openers, case studies. Start inside the action ("We almost lost this deal at the inspection…"), then circle back to fill in how we got here. Buys attention up front.

## The spine every structure shares

Whatever framework you pick, a story needs:
- **A reason to care in the first beat** — stakes, curiosity, or emotion. (Hand the opening to `hook-and-headline`.)
- **Tension or movement** — a question, a problem, a journey. Static description is not a story.
- **Specificity** — concrete, sensory, particular details. "The kitchen where you'll actually want to host Thanksgiving," not "spacious entertaining areas."
- **A satisfying close** — a payoff and, usually, a clear next step.

## Make it concrete (the part most people skip)

The difference between a story that lands and one that doesn't is almost always *detail resolution*. Trade abstract for specific every time:
- "Peaceful neighborhood" → "the kind of street where you hear the school bus, not traffic."
- "Great for entertaining" → "a kitchen island four people can actually sit around while you cook."
- "Strong play tonight" → "the model's most confident MLB edge of the week, and here's the one number driving it."

Sensory beats conceptual. Specific beats general. Particular-to-this beats true-of-anything.

## Match voice and market

Run the same structure in different registers depending on who's telling it and where:
- **Florida:** coastal, established, a little aspirational.
- **New Hampshire:** small-town, warm, neighbor-to-neighbor, trust-first (it's a brand-new market — every story is earning credibility).
- **Roofing:** practical, trustworthy, urgency-aware.
- Always preserve the *agent's* voice — I supply the structure, they keep their sound.

## Deliver

Give the piece in the chosen structure, note briefly which framework you used and why (so the agent learns the move), and keep it tight. If a piece is fighting its structure, say so and offer the better-fitting one rather than forcing it.
