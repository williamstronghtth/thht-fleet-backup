# Idea Generation

The default failure mode of any idea request — human or AI — is handing over the first plausible answer. The first answer is the one everyone already thought of. This skill is about deliberately blowing past it to find the idea worth shipping.

## The core move: diverge, then converge

Never generate and judge at the same time — judgment kills generation. Do them in sequence:

1. **Diverge.** Produce a *lot* of options without filtering. Aim for at least 15–20 for anything that matters. Let some be bad; bad ideas are stepping stones to good ones and you can't predict which.
2. **Converge.** Now switch hats. Cut hard. Pick the 2–3 strongest and sharpen them into something genuinely usable.

Deliver the converged set, not the raw dump — unless the user explicitly wants to see everything.

## Kill the first three

Before you let yourself keep any idea, generate and discard the first three that come to mind. They're the obvious ones (the cliché, the safe play, the thing the competitor already did). The interesting territory starts at idea four. State this discipline to yourself and actually do it.

## Techniques for forcing range

Rotate through these when the well runs dry. Each one cracks the problem open from a different angle:

- **Quantity sprint.** Set a number (20) and hit it fast, no editing. The back half is where the surprises live.
- **SCAMPER.** Take the obvious version and Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify/magnify, Put to another use, Eliminate, or Reverse a piece of it. Seven prompts, seven new directions.
- **Inversion.** Ask the opposite. Not "how do we make this listing appealing" but "how would we make it *un*-appealing" — then invert the answers. Surfaces assumptions you didn't know you had.
- **Forced connection / random input.** Grab an unrelated thing (a soccer formation, a fantasy-baseball stat, a Mumford lyric, a place from a GeoSports round) and force a bridge to the problem. Most won't work; the one that does is fresh precisely because nobody else would've connected them. (For systematic cross-domain transfer, use the `cross-pollination` skill.)
- **Constraint injection.** Add a hard limit: "in 6 words," "no adjectives," "for someone who hates real estate ads," "as if it were a sports broadcast." Constraints breed creativity by closing off the easy path.
- **Persona lens.** "What would [Ken Pozek / a stand-up comedian / a documentary director / a sports analyst] do with this?" Borrowed instincts unlock un-borrowed ideas.
- **Combinatory play.** Take two of your own mediocre ideas and fuse them. Two 6/10s often make one 9.

## The anti-cliché filter (apply during converge)

An idea fails this filter if:
- It could describe any other house, business, or product without changing a word (too generic).
- It uses dead language: "nestled," "hidden gem," "look no further," "your dream home awaits," "in today's market," "elevate," "unlock," "game-changer."
- The metaphor is so common it's invisible.
- It's a "tips" format where the tips are things the audience already knows.

If an idea trips the filter, either make it radically more specific or throw it out.

## The "so what" and "only us" tests

Two questions to pressure-test survivors:
- **So what?** If the audience would shrug, it's not done. Push until there's a real reason to care.
- **Could only we say this?** The best ideas are ones a competitor literally couldn't copy — because they're tied to this specific house, this specific town, this specific person, this specific data. Generic is forgettable; specific is ownable.

## How to deliver

Hand back the converged 2–3, each as: **the idea in one punchy line**, then one sentence on *why it's strong*, then (if useful) one line on *how to execute it*. Lead with the boldest option you'd actually defend. If the request was for a name or a single line, give a tight shortlist (5–7) rather than one, so the requester has range to choose from.
